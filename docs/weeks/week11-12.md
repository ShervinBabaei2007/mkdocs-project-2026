# High-Fidelity Wireframes

> **Summary:** This week I moved from structure to visual design, producing high-fidelity wireframes for the PNWX redesign. The visual decisions - typography, color, and layout - were each tied to a specific usability goal rather than personal preference. Three Nielsen Norman heuristics guided every major choice.

---

## Where This Fits in the Project

If you're jumping in here, here's the quick context: I'm redesigning [Pacific Northwest X-Ray Inc.](https://www.pnwx.com/) - a medical imaging supplier with a cluttered, hard-to-navigate site. The [low-fidelity wireframes (Week 7/8)](week7-and8.md) defined the structure. This week, I gave that structure a visual language.

---

## The Core Problem

The original site suffers from visual noise - competing colors, inconsistent styling, and skull icons on navigation links. Every element fights for attention, so nothing stands out.

My redesign strips this back to essentials and makes every visual choice functional.

---

## Visual Decisions & Their Rationale

### Typography

I paired **Raleway** (headers) with **Inter** (body text). Raleway gives the brand a distinct character; Inter is optimized for screen legibility at small sizes. Together they balance identity with readability.

## Three Heuristics Behind the Design

### Heuristic #1 - Aesthetic and Minimalist Design

Product cards show only what users need to make a decision: product image, name, key spec, and price. No decorative clutter competing with the product itself. This directly addresses the original PNWX site's habit of surrounding product information with promotional banners and unrelated links.

### Heuristic #4 - Consistency and Standards

Navigation looks and behaves the same on every page. Users build a mental model once and it holds throughout the site. The original site has three distinct navigation patterns across product, service, and resource pages - this redesign collapses them into one.

### Heuristic #6 - Recognition Over Recall

On-screen filters and sidebar categories let users find products by scanning, not by memorizing a site map. A user should never have to remember where they found something; the interface should always show them.

## What I Learned & How It Applies

Anchoring every visual decision to a heuristic solved a practical problem I hadn't anticipated: **it made disagreements resolvable**. When a color choice or layout decision felt uncertain, I could return to the question "which heuristic does this serve?" and get a clear answer. Personal preference alone would have produced a less consistent result.

The three-color system had the most direct usability impact on this specific site. PNWX's original design uses at least six competing accent colors with no consistent role assignment - blue links, yellow highlights, red warnings, orange promotions, and more. Reducing that to three colors, each with a single defined job, directly addresses Heuristic #4 (Consistency) and makes the interface legible at a glance. The test in Week 10 confirmed users moved quickly through the structure; these visual decisions are designed to preserve that speed while adding credibility and clarity.
